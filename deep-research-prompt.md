# Deep Research Brief: Fact-Check and Chart Data for "Black Bitcoiners: A Visual Book"

## Background

We're publishing "Black Bitcoiners: A Visual Book," a free, short, illustrated web book (blackbitcoiner.com) for Black Americans. It explains how money works, why the current system keeps Black families behind on wealth, and why Bitcoin and asset ownership are a way out. It has 11 short chapters in four parts: The Problem, Money, Assets, and The Way Out. A longer deluxe print edition with a workbook will follow.

The book is persuasive and written in plain, direct language, around an 8th-grade reading level. Its opinions and framing are intentional. Please don't soften or argue with them. Your job is to make sure every fact, number, date and historical claim is accurate, current, and backed by a citation we can print on the page. Critics will look for errors, so accuracy matters more than making the argument sound stronger.

Today's date is September 30, 2026. Use the most recent data available as of today and give an "as of" date for every figure.

## Research rules

1. Use primary or authoritative sources: U.S. government agencies (Census, BLS, BEA, Treasury, FDIC, USDA, SBA), the Federal Reserve system and FRED, the BIS, court records, peer-reviewed research, university presses, major museums and archives, and established industry data providers.
2. Wikipedia and blogs are fine for finding sources, but never as the final citation.
3. If a claim is wrong, overstated or outdated, say so plainly and give corrected wording. Don't quietly fix it.
4. If sources disagree, show the range and recommend which figure to use and why.
5. Never invent a number, quote, date or URL. If you can't verify something, mark it UNVERIFIED.
6. For historical claims, quote or closely paraphrase the source's own wording.
7. Flag anything that could expose us to legal or reputational risk, such as wrongly accusing a named company or person, or using copyrighted images.

## Output format

### Part A: one entry per claim, using its ID

- **ID and claim** (as written below)
- **Verdict:** CONFIRMED / CONFIRMED WITH UPDATE / NEEDS REWORDING / FALSE / UNVERIFIED
- **Verified fact:** the precise figure or fact, with its as-of date
- **Source:** publisher, title, publication date, direct URL, and the table, page or series ID
- **Supporting quote:** a short excerpt from the source, if helpful
- **Suggested wording:** one or two plain-English sentences, 30 words max, ready to put on the page
- **Short citation:** for example "Federal Reserve, Survey of Consumer Finances, 2022"
- **Notes and caveats:** anything we should know

### Part B: chart datasets

- The data as a CSV-ready table: year, value, unit
- Source, series ID (FRED IDs where they exist), and a download URL
- Reuse and license terms
- Recommended chart type and a one-line caption in plain language
- Anything a skeptic could say is misleading about the chart, and how to avoid it

### Part C: a summary table

List every claim ID with its verdict, so we can see at a glance what's safe to publish.

## Claims to verify

### Chapter 1: The Black Paradox (Black spending power vs. Black wealth)

- **1.1** The Black American population is about 47 million (Black alone or in combination with another race). Give the latest Census estimate and say which definition it uses.
- **1.2** Black Americans spend about $1.6 trillion a year (the Selig Center "buying power" estimate). Find the latest figure and its year.
- **1.3** Median household wealth is $44,900 for Black households vs. $285,000 for white households (Federal Reserve Survey of Consumer Finances, 2022). Check whether newer survey data has been published.
- **1.4** Most clothing, sneakers and fashion lose resale value right after purchase. Find data on typical resale value for clothing, sneakers and luxury goods. Identify the known exceptions, such as Hermès Birkin bags and limited sneakers, and whether they reliably beat inflation.
- **1.5** Black dandyism has a history of dressing well to challenge racial hierarchy and assert dignity. Give sources (for example Monica L. Miller, "Slaves to Fashion," 2009, and the Metropolitan Museum's 2025 exhibition "Superfine: Tailoring Black Style") and a 1–2 sentence summary.

### Chapter 2: The Two Problems (inflation and lack of assets)

- **2.1** Inflation originally meant expanding the money supply, and rising prices are the result. Document the history of the definition (older dictionaries, Austrian-school economists) and how mainstream economists define it now. We want to present the money-supply meaning accurately without overclaiming.
- **2.2** Cars lose value fast. Give typical depreciation after year one and after year five.
- **2.3** Car repossessions and delinquencies: latest counts and trends, including subprime auto loans and any breakdown by race or income.
- **2.4** Jewelry that isn't gold or another precious metal usually resells for far below retail. Include how much lab-grown diamond prices have fallen and what silver jewelry resells for compared with retail.
- **2.5** Share of U.S. adults who couldn't cover a $400 emergency expense with cash, by race if available (Fed "Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households" report). This supports a section on "F-You Money," meaning savings that give people the freedom to leave a bad job.

### Chapter 3: Historical Foundation ("How did we get here?")

- **3.1** Black Codes: dates (around 1865–1866), which states, and key provisions.
- **3.2** Convict leasing: when it started, how the 13th Amendment's exception for punishment of a crime allowed it, and when it ended (Alabama around 1928?). Include scale if it's documented.
- **3.3** Jim Crow: the standard start and end dates to use.
- **3.4** Sharecropping: the time period and how it kept Black farmers in debt.
- **3.5** The 1944 GI Bill: how local administration and banks shut Black veterans out. Verify the often-cited 1947 Mississippi figure (reportedly 2 of about 3,200 VA home loans went to Black veterans) and name the source (for example Ira Katznelson, "When Affirmative Action Was White").
- **3.6** Pigford v. Glickman: the USDA discrimination case against Black farmers, its settlement year and amount, and the later Pigford II settlement.
- **3.7** The Freedman's Savings Bank failure in 1874: about 61,000 depositors lost nearly $3 million. Verify both figures. Also check whether the bank's white trustees and managers changed its charter to allow riskier loans and investments (including railroad-related ones) before the Panic of 1873, and Frederick Douglass's role as its last president.
- **3.8** Check the existing timeline: the Freedman's Bank opened in 1865, the Tulsa massacre destroyed Greenwood ("Black Wall Street") in 1921, and federal redlining maps date from the 1930s (HOLC).

### Chapter 4: The Fiat System

- **4.1** How the Federal Reserve finances the government. Confirm the Fed cannot lend directly to the Treasury and buys government debt on the open market (quantitative easing). Give how much Treasury debt the Fed holds at its peak and now.
- **4.2** The existing timeline: the Fed was created in 1913; Executive Order 6102 (1933) required gold to be turned in; gold was repriced from $20.67 to $35 an ounce in 1934; Nixon ended dollar-to-gold convertibility in 1971; private gold ownership became legal again on December 31, 1974.
- **4.3** Credit scores and wealth: evidence on credit score gaps by race (for example the Urban Institute), and evidence that wealthy households borrow against assets (securities-based lending, "buy, borrow, die"). This supports a section asking "Would you rather have $500k in cash or an 800 credit score?"
- **4.4** Research showing economic elites shape policy more than average voters (for example Gilens & Page, 2014). This supports the line "the rich make the rules."
- **4.5** Where the 2% inflation target came from: New Zealand in 1990; when and how the Fed formally adopted it (2012?); the Fed's dual mandate from the 1977 Federal Reserve Reform Act.

### Chapter 5: Inflation, "The Silent Tax"

- **5.1** Paper currency in circulation (about $2.4 trillion?) vs. M2 money supply (about $22 trillion?). Give the latest figures and the share of M2 that's physical cash.
- **5.2** The mattress example: $10,000 held as cash from 1971 until today buys what about how much bought in 1971? Calculate with BLS CPI-U. The same $10,000 in gold at $35 an ounce is about 285.7 ounces. What is that worth at today's gold price? Our draft says "$500,000+," which may now be well under the real value.
- **5.3** U.S. food prices rose about 25% between 2019 and 2024. Verify with USDA ERS or BLS food CPI and give the exact percentage.
- **5.4** "Core" inflation leaves out food and energy, and home prices aren't counted directly in CPI (it uses owners' equivalent rent). Confirm and explain in one sentence.

### Chapter 6: Zombie Companies & Bailouts

- **6.1** Zombie companies: how many there are and what share of U.S. or global public companies they make up, under a standard definition (BIS, Federal Reserve notes, Apollo, Bloomberg or others). Give total market value or debt if available, and say which definition each source uses.
- **6.2** Bailout totals: TARP (authorized vs. actually spent, and how much was repaid), PPP (total lent, and how much was forgiven), and the Fed's emergency lending in 2008 and 2020.
- **6.3** Toys "R" Us: the 2005 leveraged buyout (buyers, debt amount), its 2018 liquidation, and jobs lost.
- **6.4** Bed Bath & Beyond: was it a private-equity victim? Our understanding is no (a public company that failed after large buybacks, activist investors and management problems). Confirm, and suggest two or three better-documented examples of companies private equity drove into bankruptcy (for example Red Lobster, Joann, Payless, Shopko, Sears), with sources.
- **6.5** Federal tax revenue and debt-interest projections for the next 10–20 years (CBO long-term outlook). This supports the question "Will taxes be higher or lower in 10–20 years?"

### Chapter 7: Deflation

- **7.1** Current U.S. federal debt (Treasury "Debt to the Penny"). Our drafts say both $37T and $40T. Give one number with a date.
- **7.2** The GDP formula (consumption + investment + government spending + net exports) and consumer spending's current share of U.S. GDP (BEA).
- **7.3** Federal interest payments on the debt as a share of the budget, and how inflation lowers the real burden of the debt (a mainstream source such as the Fed, IMF or CBO).
- **7.4** The Black Panther Party's Free Breakfast for Children Program: start date and place (January 1969, Oakland?), how many children it served at its peak, FBI targeting, and any documented link to the federal School Breakfast Program becoming permanent in 1975.

### Chapter 8: Asset Comparison

- **8.1** Cash loses buying power every year. Give the average U.S. CPI inflation rate since 1971 and since 2000, and average M2 growth over the same periods. Our draft says "3–6% a year," so tell us which measure supports which number.
- **8.2** Emergency fund guidance of 3–6 months of expenses: which authoritative sources recommend it.
- **8.3** Silver and copper boom-bust cycles: the major price peaks and crashes (for example silver in 1980 and 2011), and evidence that mining supply rises after price increases.
- **8.4** Gold confiscation: Executive Order 6102 (1933). Rules for traveling with large amounts of gold: U.S. customs declaration rules (FinCEN Form 105 applies to currency and monetary instruments; does it cover gold bullion?), and countries with strict gold import or export limits. Our draft says "It's illegal to carry 1kg of gold on flights," which we believe is wrong for the U.S. Please correct it.
- **8.5** Bitcoin seizures: documented cases of governments seizing bitcoin, such as the Silk Road and Bitfinex cases, and the total held by the U.S. government. Explain the difference between bitcoin held on exchanges and bitcoin held in self-custody. Our draft says bitcoin "can never be confiscated." Please give accurate wording.
- **8.6** HELOCs: who hires the appraiser (the lender, under appraiser-independence rules), typical time to close, and the fact that lenders can freeze or reduce credit lines (as happened in 2008 and 2020).
- **8.7** Borrowing against index funds and stock portfolios (securities-based lines of credit): how it works and typical loan-to-value limits.

### Chapter 10: Redefining Productivity

- **10.1** Plantations run like corporations: enslaved people recorded as assets on the books, daily picking quotas tracked by overseers, depreciation schedules. Main source: Caitlin Rosenthal, "Accounting for Slavery" (Harvard University Press, 2018). Give specific facts we can cite.
- **10.2** A historical image showing prices of enslaved people, reportedly held at UC Berkeley (possibly the Bancroft Library). Find the item, its catalog record, and its reproduction and licensing terms. Suggest public-domain alternatives, such as Library of Congress bills of sale or plantation ledgers.
- **10.3** A Chris Rock stand-up bit comparing how neighbors see a white woman with free time vs. a Black man with free time. Identify the special and year, and give a short, fair paraphrase. Don't reproduce long quotes.

### Chapter 11: The Bitcoin Solution

- **11.1** The 21 million bitcoin supply cap: how many bitcoin have been mined as of today, and the halving schedule.
- **11.2** Estimated lost bitcoin (Chainalysis or others).
- **11.3** "Only 21 million people could ever own one whole bitcoin." Compare that with world population (about 8.2 billion) to get the percentage. We think it's about the top 0.26%, not "top 1%." Also give an estimate of how many addresses or entities currently hold 1 BTC or more, with the caveat that addresses aren't people.
- **11.4** Holder tier names and cutoffs (for example Shrimp under 1 BTC, Crab, Octopus, Fish, Dolphin, Shark, Whale, Humpback), as defined by Glassnode or another standard source.
- **11.5** Unbanked and underbanked rates by race (the FDIC National Survey of Unbanked and Underbanked Households, latest year).
- **11.6** Historical record: for all 4-year holding periods since, for example, 2013, what share ended with a gain? Give the method used and a clear "past performance doesn't guarantee future results" caveat.
- **11.7** Circular Bitcoin economies: documented examples (for example Bitcoin Beach in El Salvador, Bitcoin Ekasi in South Africa, and any Black-led U.S. community projects), with sources.
